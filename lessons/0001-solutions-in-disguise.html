Solutions in disguise

Most project ideas show up already dressed as solutions. The first skill is spotting that.

≈ 10 minutesMindset shift 1 of 3: solutions → problemsSource: IDG Module 1
Great digital products start with problems, not solutions.
Institute of Digital Government, Understanding the Problem

IDG's point is that a project can fail even when it's built well: “Projects can struggle even when execution is strong, especially when they are solving the wrong problem.”[1] Good execution doesn't rescue a badly chosen problem. Their fix: “diagnose the real problem first, then decide what to build.”

The trouble is that ideas rarely arrive as problems. They arrive as things to build.

The tell

A solution in disguise names an artefact: an app, a chatbot, a dashboard, “use AI to…”. It sounds like a problem because it's phrased as a need (“we need a…”), but it has already skipped the diagnosis.

A problem names who is struggling, with what, and ideally what it costs them. It says nothing about the fix.

Solution in disguise“We need a chatbot for our help page.”
Problem“Customers email us the same five questions every day, and each reply takes staff ten minutes.”

(Our example, not IDG's.)

Look at what the second version opens up. Maybe a chatbot is the answer. Maybe it's rewriting one confusing paragraph on the help page, which costs an afternoon, not a project.

Why this matters when you scope projects

Once the problem is clear, the first solution can be tiny. IDG's health appointment booking system didn't start as a booking system. It started as a FormSG form feeding into Excel, which was deliberately unscalable but cheap enough to prove people wanted it.[2] You can only get away with something that small if you know which problem you're testing.

Practice: problem or solution?

Sort each statement. Watch out: some solutions sound like needs.

“We should build a dashboard so managers can see ticket volumes.”

“Parents miss enrolment deadlines because the notice is buried on page 7 of a PDF.”

“Let's use AI to summarise incoming customer emails.”

“New volunteers stop turning up after their first session, and we don't know why.”

“Our clinic needs an online booking app.”

“Staff spend about three hours a week retyping the same data into two systems.”

Your turn: unwrap one

Take “Our clinic needs an online booking app” and rewrite it as a problem. You'll have to invent a plausible situation, and that's fine. Then tick off the checklist.

Show one possible answer

“Working patients can only book by phone during office hours, when they're at work. Many give up, and the clinic sees about 15 no-shows or walk-ins a day that it can't plan staff around.”

Notice how this already hints at cheaper fixes than an app: an after-hours voicemail, a simple form, longer phone hours. It also fits IDG's point that “many successful products started by solving a specific problem for a defined group of users”[1], here working patients rather than “patients”.
